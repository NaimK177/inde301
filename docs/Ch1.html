1.1 Steps for a study

Real-world engineering problems are rarely neatly packaged with all the necessary numbers provided. Before jumping into compound interest formulas and cash flow diagrams, an engineer must understand the broader context of decision-making. Tackling a capital investment problem requires a systematic, structured approach to ensure no critical factors are overlooked.

The standard procedure for an engineering economy study consists of the following nine steps:

  1. 1.

    Identify and understand the problem; define the objective11 1 “A problem well stated is a problem half solved” - Charles Kettering.

  2. 2.

    Collect relevant data and information.

  3. 3.

    Develop alternatives and estimate costs

  4. 4.

    Define the evaluation criteria 22 2 We focus in this course on economic criteria (e.g.: present worth, future worth, rate of return, payback period, etc.) However, non-economic factors (such as resource availability, environmental impact) are also important

  5. 5.

    Evaluate each alternative

  6. 6.

    Select the best alternative

  7. 7.

    Implement the solution

  8. 8.

    Monitor the results

  9. 9.

    refine the solution (go back to 3)

To better understand this workflow, we can group these nine steps into three distinct phases:

Phase 1: The Foundation (Steps 1–3)

This is often the most difficult and time-consuming part of a project. A perfectly executed mathematical analysis is useless if it solves the wrong problem.

  • •

    Gathering Data: In textbooks, data is perfectly packaged for you. In the real world, Step 2 requires digging through historical maintenance logs, calling vendors for equipment quotes, and projecting future inflation rates.

  • •

    Alternatives: Step 3 requires creativity. Remember that the “Do Nothing” (status quo) option is almost always a valid alternative that must be evaluated against new investments.

Phase 2: Mathematical Modelling and Evaluation (Steps 4–6)

This phase involves translating physical alternatives (like building a new facility versus upgrading an old one) into a timeline of expected cash inflows and outflows. Once the costs and cash flows are mapped, we apply our chosen economic criteria (Step 4) to evaluate them fairly (Step 5) and objectively select the best path forward (Step 6).

Phase 3: Action and Accountability (Steps 7–9)

Engineering does not end when a decision is made. Once management selects and implements the best alternative, engineers must track its real-world performance. For example, if a new machine was estimated to save $2,000 a month in electricity, Step 8 involves checking the actual utility bills a year later. Step 9 uses that feedback to either refine the current operation or improve the accuracy of future estimates.

While engineering economy principles are applied most extensively in Steps 2 through 6, a successful professional engineer must be prepared to engage with the entire lifecycle of a project.

1.2 Time Value of Money

“It is a well-known fact that money makes money. The time value of money explains the change in the amount of money over time for funds that are owned (invested) or owed (borrowed). This is the most important concept in engineering economy.”

If we invest money, we expect to have the original amount plus some interest. If we borrow money, we expect to return the original amount plus some interest.

Money possesses a time value

Interest and inflation are the most significant drivers of the Time Value of Money.

1.5 Simple and Compound Interest

So far we have analyzed problem over a single interest period. When we have multiple periods, we need to differentiate two definitions of interest.

Definition: Simple vs. Compound Interest

Simple interest is calculated using the principal only, while compound interest is calculated on the principal plus the total amount of interest accumulated in all previous period.

1.5.1 Simple interest

With simple interest, the borrower pays interest only on the borrowed amount (the principal). Let P be the borrowed amount, n be the number of periods, and i be the interest rate per period. The total amount F the borrower pays back n periods later is:

F =P+i⁢P+⋯+i⁢P⏟n⁢ times
=P+n⁢i⁢P
=P⁢(1+n⁢i)

In practice, simple interest is rarely used because it ignores the time value of money. Lenders expect a return on all capital tied up in a loan—including unpaid interest—making compound interest the commercial standard.

1.5.2 Compound Interest

With compound interest, the borrower pays on the borrowed amount plus interest accumulated in previous periods. In other words, the borrower pays interest on the interest.

Let Ft be the future value at the end of period t. The future value at the end of the first period is:

F1=P+P⁢i=P⁢(1+i)

End of Period 2:

F2=F1+F1⁢i=F1⁢(1+i)=P⁢(1+i)⁢(1+i)=P⁢(1+i)2

End of period 3:

F3=F2⁢(1+i)=P⁢(1+i)2⁢(1+i)=P⁢(1+i)3

End of Period n:

Fn=Fn−1⁢(1+i)=P⁢(1+i)n−1⁢(1+i)=P⁢(1+i)n
Example:

A manufacturing facility plans to upgrade its production line by purchasing an automated packaging machine. The plant manager needs to secure a loan of $100,000 for a duration of 5 years and is evaluating two different financing offers.

  • •

    A specialized industrial financing firm offers the loan at an annual compound interest rate of 6.0%.

P =$100,000is=0.085⁢ per yearn=5⁢ years

=100,000⁢(1+5×0.085)
=100,000⁢(1.425)=$142,500

The total interest paid to the private investor is $42,500.

Option B: Specialized Industrial Bank (Compound Interest)

ic =0.06⁢ per year
Fcompound =P⁢(1+ic)n

Definition: Cash Flows

Cash flows are the amounts of money estimated for future projects or observed for project events that have taken place

Definition: Cash Outflows

Cash outflows are costs, disbursements, expenses, and taxes caused by projects and business activity.
Amount of funds flowing out of a firm.
A negative sign indicates a cash outflow.

Example of cash outflows:

  • •

  • •

  • •

Remark: Point vs. Range Estimates

We can note that future costs could be given in the form of a point estimate or a range estimate. The main criteria for which kind of estimate to give depends on how confident the estimates are. Higher confidence in the estimates make the use of a point estimate valid. However, as soon as an estimate cannot be provided accurately, point estimates are not preferred. For instance, this could be caused by the lack of knowledge and expertise of the person doing the estimation, or it could be because of the uncertainties in external factors, such as shipping.

Once all cash inflows and outflows are estimated (or determined for a completed project), the net cash flow for each time period is calculated:

Net flow = cash inflows - cash outflows

1.7 Minimum Attractive Rate of Return

For any investment to be profitable, the investor (corporate or individual) expects to receive more money than the amount of capital invested. In other words, a fair rate of return (ROR), or return on investment, must be realizable.

Remember that the definition of ROR is the amount earned divided by the principal:

Rate of return (%) =interest accrued per time unitprincipal×100%
=total investment value−principalprincipal×100%

Alternatives are evaluated upon the projection that a reasonable ROR can be expected44 4 We expect something to happen because we are uncertain of the outcome of the investment.

Definition: Minimum Attractive Rate of Return (MARR)

The Minimum Attractive Rate of Return (MARR) is a reasonable rate of return established for the evaluation and selection of alternatives. A project is not economically viable unless it is expected to return at least the MARR. MARR is also referred to as the hurdle rate, cutoff rate, benchmark rate, and minimum acceptable rate of return.

The MARR is usually a policy issue resolved by the top management of an organization in view of numerous considerations. Among these considerations are the following:

  1. 1.

    Cost and Source of Capital: The amount of money available for investment, and whether those funds are equity55 5 A company uses its own assets to finance, often through issuing stocks or debt66 6 A company borrows money to finance, often through issuing bonds.

  2. 2.

    Project Portfolio: The number of good projects available and their urgency (i.e., whether they sustain essential present operations or are elective expansions).

  3. 3.

    Project Risk: The amount of perceived risk associated with the investment, along with the administrative costs over short versus long planning horizons. Riskier projects require a higher MARR.

  4. 4.

    Organization Type: Whether the entity is a government, public utility, or private industry (governments typically have much lower MARRs than private startups).

Why isn’t the MARR just 0%?

You might wonder: If a project makes even 1% profit, isn’t that better than nothing? Why set the hurdle so high?

Companies set the MARR higher than zero because money isn’t free. For example, if a company has $100,000, they could simply put it in a guaranteed savings account earning 5% interest, yielding $5,000 per year risk-free. Therefore, any project they invest that $100,000 into must return significantly more than 5% to justify the risk and effort. If a proposed project only returns 1%, it is a net loss compared to just leaving the money in the bank.

If you want to purchase a new widescreen HDTV, but do not have sufficient money (capital), you could choose one of the following alternatives:

  • •

    Obtain a bank loan for, say, a cost of capital of 9% per year and pay for the TV in cash now.

  • •

    Use your credit card and pay off the balance on a monthly basis. This approach will probably cost you at least 15% per year.

  • •

    Use funds from your savings account that earns 5% per year and pay cash. This approach means that you also forgo future returns from these funds77 7 In engineering economy, this lost potential return is called an opportunity cost.

The 9%, 15%, and 5% rates are your cost of capital estimates to raise the capital for the system by different methods of capital financing.

Definition: Sources of Capital

In general, capital is developed in two ways:

Equity financing:

The firm uses its own money. This includes cash on hand or selling partial ownership of the company (issuing stocks).

Debt financing:

The firm uses borrowed money. This includes taking loans from a bank or borrowing from investors with a promise to pay them back with interest (issuing bonds).

The MARR is set such that:

MARR > cost of capital

Combining these two ways is widely done in practice. For instance, you can buy the TV with 40% credit card money at 15% per year and 60% from the savings funds that is earning you 5% per year. In that particular example, we calculate the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) as follows:

WACC =(0.40×15%)+(0.60×5%)
=6%+3%
=9%⁢ per year

Therefore, your blended cost of capital is 9%, and your MARR must be set higher than this threshold.

A project is viable if and only if its ROR ≥ MARR.

1.8 The Rule of 72: A Mental Math Shortcut

While modern engineering economy relies on precise compound interest formulas and software, professionals often need a way to evaluate investments rapidly during meetings or site visits. For this, we use the Rule of 72.

Definition: Rule of 72

The Rule of 72 is a mathematical approximation used to estimate how long it will take for an investment (or a debt) to exactly double in value at a given fixed annual compound interest rate.

Instead of solving the logarithmic compound interest equation, you simply divide the number 72 by the annual interest rate (expressed as a whole number, not a decimal):

Years to Double≈72Interest Rate

Example: If an industrial project guarantees a 9% annual rate of return, how long will it take for the invested capital to double?

Years to Double≈729=8⁢ years

(Note: The exact mathematical answer is 8.04 years. The Rule of 72 is remarkably accurate for typical interest rates between 5% and 12%.)

When to Use the Rule of 72

The Rule of 72 is an estimation tool, meaning it should never be used for final engineering budgets or formal project approvals.

However, it is highly valuable in the following scenarios:

  • •

    Initial Project Screening: If a manager states that a $1 million automation upgrade must double its value in 5 years, the engineer instantly knows the project needs to yield roughly 72/5=14.4% annually. If the best projection is only 8%, the project is immediately rejected without wasting time on detailed spreadsheets.

  • •

    Understanding the ”Weight” of the MARR: Stating a corporate MARR is “12%” can feel abstract. Using the Rule of 72 (72/12=6), managers translate this into a tangible reality: Any approved project must double its money every 6 years.

  • •

    Evaluating the Danger of Debt: It quickly highlights the risk of high-interest financing. A credit card used to buy equipment at 18% annual interest will double the debt owed in just 4 years (72/18=4).
