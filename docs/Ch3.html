Chapter 3 Nominal and Effective Interest Rates

In the previous chapter, we established equivalence factors assuming that the interest is compounded once a year. However, in practical scenarios, interest is often compounded more frequently (e.g., daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, or continuously). Because of this more frequent compounding, the actual interest accumulated over a year will be greater than what a simple stated rate implies. Therefore, we must formally distinguish between the stated (nominal) interest rate and the true (effective) interest rate.

Example:

Consider a principal of $100 to be invested for one year at an interest rate of 1% per month.

Calculate the amount of interest and the annual interest rate.

  • •

    F=P⁢(1+i)n=100⁢(1.01)12=$112.68. The interest earned is $12.68.

  • •

    The actual annual interest rate is 12.68/100=12.68%.

We say that the effective annual interest rate is 12.68%, and the nominal interest rate is 12%.

3.1 Nominal Interest Rate

Definition: Nominal Interest Rate r (APR)

A nominal interest rate r (often referred to in consumer finance as the Annual Percentage Rate or APR) is a rate that does not consider compounding.

r=interest rate per period×number of periods (3.1)

Note: Annual Percentage Rate (APR)

Nominal interest rate is commonly referred to as Annual Percentage Rate “APR”. While nominal rates can theoretically be calculated for any duration, in practice and in most problems in this course, “nominal rate” assumes an annual period (APR).

3.2 Effective Interest Rate

Definition: Effective Interest Rate

An effective interest rate i is a rate that does consider compounding.

The effective interest rate is the actual or observed rate that applies for a period of time. In financial contracts, interest rates are frequently stated in the following format:

r (per year), compounded every C⁢P

where C⁢P is the compounding period (e.g., yearly, monthly, weekly) From this statement, we must calculate the actual effective interest rate that applies to each compounding period.

Note: Annual Percentage Yield (APY)

The effective interest rate i is called the Annual Percentage Yield “APY”.

3.2.1 Computing the Effective Interest Rate

Given that the effective interest rate is the actual rate in the compounding period, then

i=rm

where m=1/C⁢P is the compounding frequency per year11 1 Given that CP is a fraction of a year.. For instance, a nominal interest rate of 12% (r=12%) compounded monthly (CP=1/12 and m=12) translates into an effective rate of 1% per month.

Let ia be the effective annual rate. By setting the future worth F calculated with the effective annual rate equal to the future worth calculated with the compounded nominal rate, we can isolate ia:

F=P⁢(1+ia)=P⁢(1+rm)m

As a result, we can compute ia as follows:

P⁢(1+ia) =P⁢(1+rm)m
1+ia =(1+rm)m
ia =(1+rm)m−1
Definition: Effective Annual Interest Rate Formula

The effective annual interest rate is:

ia=(1+rm)m−1

3.3 Factors under m-time-a-year Compounding

In the previous chapter, we discussed engineering economy factors. These factors used the interest rate i per compounding period, that is r/m. When expressed with r and m, the Future Worth becomes:

F=P⁢(1+r/m)k

where k is the total number of periods. Hence, the Future Worth Factor F/P can be written as:

(F/P,r,m,k)=(1+r/m)k

and (F/P,r,m,k)⇔(F/P,i,k).

Similarly, the Present Worth factor P/F can be written using the nominal rate and compounding frequency as follows:

(P/F,r,m,k)=(1+r/m)−k

and (P/F,r,m,k)⇔(P/F,i,k).

3.4 Continuous Compounding

If we allow compounding to occur more and more frequently, the compounding period (CP) becomes shorter and shorter and m, the number of compounding periods per payment period, increases. Let C⁢P→0. Then m=1/C⁢P→∞. This situation is known as continuous compounding22 2 Continuous compounding appears in options pricing and certain financial models, but for physical engineering projects, monthly or quarterly compounding is the practical limit..

Definition: Continuous Compounding

Continuous compounding is present when the duration of CP, the compounding period, becomes infinitely small and m, the number of times interest is compounded per period, becomes infinite.

As compounding becomes continuous, we rely on the natural logarithm base e to model continuous growth. Given the definition of Euler’s number e:

limh→∞(1+1h)h=e

we can write the effective annual interest rate as:

limm→∞i =limm→∞(1+rm)m−1
=limh→∞(1+1h)h⁢r−1
=er−1

by taking r/m=1/h and consequently m=h⁢r.

Definition: Effective Rate Under Continuous Compounding

Given a nominal annual rate r%, the effective annual interest rate is:

i=er−1
Important: Growth Factor Under Continuous Compounding

Under continuous compounding, the rate of growth of an investment over a time t expressed in years, i.e., the F/P factor, is

limm→∞(1+r/m)m⁢t=er⁢t

3.4.1 Factors

Since i=er−1, we can substitute i in the factors developed in Chapter 2 to find their corresponding continuous compounding factors.

For instance, the continuous counterpart of P/F=(1+i)−n under continuous compounding is:

P/F=e−r⁢t

To keep discrete and continuous time different, we use t for continuous time, and n for discrete time.

3.5 Bonds

Definition: Bond

A bond is a loan or an IOU (I Owe You). A bond is a long-term note issued by a corporation or a government entity (the borrower) to finance major projects.

It represents the major source that governments and companies use to obtain debt financing. “The distinguishing characteristic of a bond is that the borrower (the issuer) enters into a legal agreement to compensate the lender (the lender, the bondholder) through periodic interest payments in the form of coupons; and to repay the original sum (the principal) in full on a stipulated date, which is known as the bond’s “maturity date.”” [thau_bond_2011].

Important: Bond Market Price Variation

A bond can be traded freely in the marketplace. Its price varies continuously.

3.5.2 Cashflow

The cashflow for a bond investment is conventional, as shown in Figure 3.3. Because the cash flows of a bond are locked in by a legal contract, the only way for the open market to adjust to changing economic conditions is to change the upfront Purchase Price (P). To determine what a fair purchase price should be today, an investor must evaluate those fixed future cash flows against the interest rates currently available in the broader economy. This introduces a critical new characteristic: the yield.

3.5.3 Yield to Maturity

Imagine buying a $1,000 bond that pays $50 a year (a 5% coupon). A year later, the economy shifts and new $1,000 bonds are issued paying $100 a year (a 10% return). If you try to sell your original bond for $1,000, no one will buy it:

why buy yours for 50 a year when they can get 100 a year?

The present value of your bond is lower because the market has shifted. To sell your bond, you must offer it at a “discount” (e.g., selling it for 800). The buyer pays less upfront, which increases their yield (their actual rate of return) to match the new 10% market standard.

Definition: Yield to Maturity (YTM)

Yield to Maturity (YTM) is an estimate of the total rate of return anticipated to be earned by an investor who buys a bond at a given market price, holds it to maturity, and receives all interest payments and the capital redemption on schedule.

[thau_bond_2011]

Unlike coupon rate, the yield is a changing variable determined by the changes of the market:

  • •

    Coupon Rate: Fixed at issuance, determined by the borrower (issuer), represents the legal promise of cash flow. It NEVER changes.

  • •

    Yield to Maturity (YTM): Variable, determined by the open market (investors), represents the current opportunity cost or required return. It changes every day based on the economy.

Consider a bond with a price of P and a face value F, making m coupon payments per year of C/m (with a total of n payments). See the respective cashflow in Figure 3.3.

The Yield to Maturity (YTM), denoted as λ, is the interest rate that equates the present worth of the bond’s future cash flows to its current market price P. Using standard engineering economy equivalence factors, the price of the bond P is the sum of the present worth of the face value and the present worth of the uniform coupon series:

P=F⁢(P/F,λ/m,n)+Cm⁢(P/A,λ/m,n) (3.2)

Where:

  • •

    F⁢(P/F,λ/m,n) brings the single Face Value payment back to year 0.

  • •

    Cm⁢(P/A,λ/m,n) brings the uniform series of coupon payments back to year 0.

By expanding these factors into their standard formulas (from Chapter 2), we get the full equation for the price of a bond:

P=F⁢(1+λm)−n+Cλ⁢(1−1(1+λ/m)n) (3.3)

This formulation clearly shows that as the required yield λ increases, the denominator of the present worth factors increases. Therefore, the price of the bond P decreases as its yield increases. That is, a high-yield bond will have a “low” price from the investor’s point of view, but why?

Note: Algebraic Derivation of the YTM Formula

For those interested in the mathematics, the uniform coupon series is a geometric sum:

∑k=1nC/m(1+λ/m)k

Using the mathematical identity for geometric series, ∑k=1n1(1+x)k=1−(1+x)−nx, and substituting x=λ/m, the summation algebraically reduces exactly to the (P/A) portion of Equation 3.3.

Important: Yield vs. Coupon Rate Relationship

Remember that the yield represents the broader market behaviour. When the yield is higher than the coupon rate, the present value of the bond becomes smaller than its face value because the market is providing higher rates of return.

This behaviour is illustrated in Figure 3.4 where the present value of the bond is exactly equal its par value when the yield and coupon rate are equal (10%).

What is the price of a 10% (coupon, paid semi-annually), 30-year US Treasury bond with a yield of 4%? Assume a face value of 100. (Bond prices are typically quoted as percentage of face value.)

=100⁢(1+0.042)−60+100.04⁢(1−1(1+0.04/2)60)

3.6 Time Varying Interest Rate

In practice, interest rates may vary from one period to the other. In particular, it is often expected that the interest rate will increase with time. This fact is reflected in the annual yield on long (maturity) bonds being higher than that on short bonds, as shown in Table 3.5.

If the interest rates in periods, 1,…,n are i1,…,in, then, the Future worth F of a Present amount P after n periods is:

F=P⁢(1+i1)⁢(1+i2)⁢…⁢(1+in)=P⁢∏k=1n(1+ik)

The rates i1,…,in are known as short rates. The short rate in represents the expected 1-year rate after n years, and their value is estimated based on the yields of bonds (similar to the ones on Table 3.5).

3.7 Summary

This chapter expanded the foundational equivalence concepts by introducing the critical distinction between nominal interest rates (the stated, uncompounded rate) and effective interest rates (the true, compounded rate). Furthermore, we explored how time-varying interest rates require a period-by-period analysis, highlighting the limitations of standard formulas when market conditions fluctuate.
